# Character art production

Produce memorable game characters and reusable construction, not a library of nearly identical portraits or generic meshes counted as finished races. This skill specializes the character decisions; the repository's existing tools remain the implementation owners.

## Establish the character before making art

Read the current consumer design authority, character record, approved images and source manifests. A conversation approval applies to the exact pictured variant, not every later derivative. Locate and inspect the closest approved single-character image before asking for stylistic continuity; do not substitute a verbal style description for an available visual reference.

For a new notable, establish a coherent memorable trope, battlefield role, silhouette, signature equipment and short narrative motive. Make powers implementable actions with targets and effects. Use the game's specified ability/passive structure rather than inventing a second combat design. Avoid arbitrary combinations of occupations and aesthetics that lack a readable character concept. Ordinary generated NPCs need distinctive appearance and useful variation, not a full authored-hero biography each.

Record what is fixed and what may vary: identity, adult age where romance applies, facial landmarks, physique, species anatomy, faction, period cuts, palette, weapon and pose. Do not invent character names or overturn approved designs merely to fill a roster. Sexualized romance styling is for adults; an NPC inventory entry alone does not imply romance eligibility.

## Keep the worlds and factions distinct

DragonRealms stops at early-modern/pirate-age clothing and has no guns, even when a gun is historically early-modern. Verify its races, guilds and creatures against the actual consumer records. Preserve exact creature variants; do not merge them by the last word of a name. Leave unsupported anatomy and sex unassessed rather than manufacturing facts.

Pirate Island deliberately permits clashing faction aesthetics. Michael's approved 1870s steampunk does not make everyone steampunk. Colonials, pirates, fox people, elves and Cthulhu need their own garment cuts, silhouettes, materials and equipment. Shared topology and fitting infrastructure must not flatten those differences. Read current faction documents for details instead of extrapolating a whole faction from a generic cloak or a palette swap.

## Build on suitable free sources

Search the shared catalogue and downloaded sources before generating again. Reuse inspected, licensed geometry, body targets, clothing patterns and compatible rigs. Preserve original bytes and source hashes; put transformations in reproducible tooling. A service having a free browser tier does not mean its API is free. Do not spend generation credits without specific authorization; reserve paid work for an agreed high-value asset and bounded polish plan.

Do not build final organic anatomy from piled primitives. Use appropriate source topology, actual mesh construction and sculptable forms. A reptilian head, digitigrade leg or arthropod body is a topology/rig problem, not a recolored human. Reusable humanoids and genuinely separate creature families are complementary parts of one production system, not competing implementations.

For source fitting, rigging, wardrobe profiles or export work, read [the implementation reference](references/shared-builder.md). Extend those owners rather than copying the editor or recipe pipeline into a consumer.

## Construct clothing, not painted modern costumes

Choose the actual cut and how it is worn: seams, panels, closures, sleeve/leg shapes, hem, layering and equipment attachment. A modern shirt with a period texture is still a modern shirt. Keep garment length independent from shoulder fit; changing a long cloak into a short one must not stretch its yoke down the torso.

Fit garments in a common body coordinate system. Carry relevant body changes into clothing, footwear and species attachments. Explicitly author covered body regions and layer exclusions. Do not hide arbitrary skin until an intersection disappears, center each part independently, or claim arbitrary separates work when only one paired outfit has been fitted.

Face, hair, body and clothes must vary without losing character identity. Keep species/sex/height decisions explicit unless the requested population generator owns them. Names, allegiance, romance state and population counts belong to the consumer simulation, not the shared appearance exporter.

## Review images, not just test results

Render the actual resulting asset in the game/editor camera, then inspect front, back and three-quarter views plus the intended gameplay scale. A reference image or successful import does not prove the model resembles it.

Check face appeal and adult read, silhouette, anatomy, period cut, shoulder/waist/hip fit, hand and weapon attachment, hair collision, lining/hem, alpha artifacts and shading. Exercise meaningful body extremes and relevant joint poses. The rest pose alone cannot certify a skirt, cloak or coat for combat animation. Record secondary-motion limits rather than claiming a rigid cape has cloth simulation.

When a render fails, identify the specific cause and change that cause before regenerating a large batch. Do not approve plain blockouts because automated assertions passed. Preserve requested variants with provenance, but admit only reviewed candidates to runtime random pools.

## Leave reusable production artifacts

Keep source, construction prototype, approved editable asset and optimized runtime output distinct. Record source/license, input hashes, tool/compiler version, transformations, approved reference, fit/visual evidence and current status. Refresh derived receipts after changing construction. Bundle exports without editor cameras, UI or accidental external dependencies; check them in a fresh load.

Use the real requirements inventory to expose missing bodies, wardrobe and monsters. Do not count a name, metadata record or generic fallback as completed coverage. Commit coherent changes through the existing repository history; report local, pushed and merged status separately. The skill does not authorize paid generation or publication beyond the user's task.
